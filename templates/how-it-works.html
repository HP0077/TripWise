02 Where the training data comes from

The Stravl Travel Preference Dataset holds 80,301 survey responses. Each person answered the same questionnaire you just did, then swiped yes or no on destinations. TripWise learns which answers go with which yes-swipes.

How the 225 were chosen

Every yes-swipe becomes one training row: the person's answers are the inputs and the destination is the label. A person who liked five places gives five rows.

Places need at least 15 yes-swipes to stay in. Within each continent the most-swiped ones are kept: 45 each for Europe, Asia and North America, and 30 each for South America, Africa and Oceania.

The cap matters. Without it, European and US cities would take most of the list, and the model would rarely recommend anywhere else.

03 Turning answers into numbers

Trees can only split on numbers, so each questionnaire becomes a row of {{ facts.feature_columns|length }} numbers. Pick an example traveller and watch the row change. Hover a cell to see what it means.

Hover or tap a cell.

Why it's encoded this way

Pick-one answers stay as a single number (budget 0 to 3, season 0 to 3, and so on). Budget and pace are ordered scales, so a split like "budget is 2 or more" means something. A skipped answer is stored as −1.

Pick-any answers become one 0-or-1 column per option (multi-hot encoding with scikit-learn's MultiLabelBinarizer). Ticking Beach and Food sets two of the eight experience columns to 1.

The continent group has eight columns, not six. The survey also offered Caribbean and Middle East, so the model knows those columns, but this site doesn't ask about them.

04 Two forests, one vote

Both models are collections of decision trees. A decision tree asks yes/no questions about your answers until it reaches a leaf, and the leaf holds odds for all 225 places. The two models differ in how they grow their trees. TripWise averages what they say.

Balanced class weights make a yes-swipe on a rarely swiped place count for more than one on a popular place. Without them the model drifts towards the same famous cities for everyone.

Depth, leaf size and tree count were set by hand, not by a search. They keep the saved model small enough for GitHub and a free Render server, and fast enough to answer in milliseconds. Deeper trees would mostly memorise individual travellers.

05 How well it works

The test set is {{ "{:,}".format(metrics.n_test_users) }} travellers the model never saw while training. For {{ "%.1f"|format(hit10) }}% of them, at least one of their top ten suggestions was a place they actually swiped yes on. Recommending the most popular places to everyone gets {{ "%.1f"|format(m.popularity_baseline['hit_rate@10'] * 100) }}%.

Share of travellers with at least one yes in their top 10

95% margin of error around TripWise's score

The vote is no better than either model alone. Random Forest alone, Extra Trees alone and the ensemble all land inside the same margin of error. Combining them didn't add accuracy on this data, but it doesn't cost any either.

Why the test set is split by traveller

One traveller contributes a row for every place they liked, and every one of those rows has the same answers. With a random row-by-row split, the same person ends up in both training and test, and the model gets graded on people it has already seen.

evaluate.py uses scikit-learn's GroupShuffleSplit to hold out 10% of travellers completely. It trains the same model on the other 90% and ranks all 225 places for each held-out traveller.

06 Which answers actually matter

To test a question, its answers were shuffled between test travellers and hit rate @10 measured again. A big drop means the model relies on that question. Four questions matter. The other four make no measurable difference.

Drop in hit rate @10 when a question's answers are shuffled (percentage points)

Spread over 5 shuffles (±2 standard deviations). Grey bars sit within noise of zero.

Continent matters most, partly because it narrows the field so much. Popularity and budget come next. Shuffling pace, safety, experiences or season doesn't measurably change the results. In this data, how much people want to spend and how far off the map they want to go say more about where they'll go than what they want to do there.

07 Limits worth knowing

  • Taste is noisy. The top pick was a place the traveller liked only {{ "%.1f"|format(m.tripwise_ensemble['hit_rate@1'] * 100) }}% of the time. Treat the list as a starting point, not a verdict.
  • 225 of {{ "{:,}".format(facts.survey_destinations) }} places. Destinations with few swipes were left out, so some great places can never come up.
  • The continent filter comes after the model. Caribbean islands count as North America and the Middle East counts as Asia, following the country lookup.
  • Photos come from a Wikipedia search. Usually right, but occasionally a city shares a name with somewhere else.
  • The survey reflects Stravl's users. Their tastes won't match everyone's.
